Start with the gap between salary and pay

The number in a job offer is the gross figure. What you live on is what remains after federal tax, state tax, FICA, your commute and the unpaid hours that come with a salaried role. On a $70,000 salary in a state with income tax, that gap is routinely $20,000 a year or more.

  • Gross pay is what the offer letter says.
  • Take-home pay is what reaches your account after withholding.
  • Effective pay subtracts commuting and unpaid extra hours. This is the number that decides whether a job is worth taking.

Common questions

Why is my take-home pay so much lower than my salary?

Three things are usually taken out before you see anything: federal income tax, state income tax where it applies, and FICA (Social Security at 6.2% and Medicare at 1.45%). On top of that, pre-tax deductions such as health premiums and retirement contributions reduce taxable income but also reduce the cash you receive. Someone on $70,000 filing single with no state income tax takes home roughly $56,000–58,000 after FICA and federal tax alone.

Is a higher salary in an expensive city actually better?

Often not. A $95,000 offer in a high-cost city can leave you worse off than $78,000 somewhere cheaper once rent, state income tax and commuting are counted. The comparison is not the salary, it is what remains after the costs the job forces on you. Use the offer comparison tool and enter the commuting distance for both, because commute cost is the item people most often leave out.

How do I compare an hourly job with a salaried one?

Convert both to the same basis. Take the salary, divide it by the hours you will actually work including the unpaid extra hours a salaried role usually carries, then compare that figure with the hourly rate. Then account for overtime eligibility: hourly workers are generally entitled to overtime pay, salaried workers above the exemption threshold are not, which can reverse the result entirely.

Are these figures accurate enough to negotiate with?

Treat them as a strong estimate, not a filing-grade calculation. Payroll withholding depends on your W-4 entries, pre-tax deductions, local taxes and mid-year changes in a way a general calculator cannot fully capture. Use the output to understand the shape of an offer and to ask better questions; verify anything contractual with the employer's payroll team or a tax professional.
